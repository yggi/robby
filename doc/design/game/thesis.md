# The thesis — you write a plan, then you watch what your plan does

Not "coding" in the sense of syntax. One idea, over and over:

> You write a plan. Then you watch what your plan actually does.

The gap between intention and execution is where all programming lives, and it
is legible to a four-year-old the moment a robot walks confidently into a wall.

## How the loop makes the gap visible

**You plan, then you run.** The plan is a strip of arrows. Pressing play is a
commitment, and the world is frozen until you make it. There is no live driving
mode and there never should be — the whole subject disappears the moment the
child can steer.

**The ghost path previews the plan.** By the time a child presses play they have
usually already seen where it goes, so play stops being "will this work?" and
becomes "I want to watch it happen." That sounds like it would remove the
tension; it does the opposite. It moves the interesting moment earlier, into the
strip, which is where the thinking is.

**One instruction can buy a lot of travel.** A corridor bought by a single arrow
is drawn as one continuous stroke. That is abstraction, shown rather than
explained, and it is why the planning vectors must never be drawn as a dot per
tile — see [`rules.md`](rules.md).

**Failure is diagnostic, not punitive.** The wrong slot is ringed and stays
ringed. The robot puts itself back. Nothing is lost, so a wrong answer is
information rather than a cost, and a child will spend wrong answers freely.

## The escalation is of the kind of thinking

Worlds escalate what you have to *think about*, not how many tokens you have to
place:

1. follow a path
2. choose between paths
3. work with machinery that moves you
4. work out an order of operations

Token count grows as a side effect of that and is never the point. A room that
is longer but asks nothing new is a worse room than a short one that asks
something, and the solver is what tells the difference —
[`../code/solver.md`](../code/solver.md).

## The evidence that it landed

A five-year-old solving the game **made the arrow shapes with her hand as she
reasoned**, tracing the direction in the air before reaching for the token.
Nobody taught her that and nothing in the game asks for it. She was not driving
the robot; she was rehearsing a plan away from the board and then committing it
— which is this page, arriving without a word of text.
[`playtest.md`](playtest.md).
